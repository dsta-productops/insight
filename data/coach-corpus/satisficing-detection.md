# Satisficing Detection

## Principle

Satisficing (Krosnick, from Simon's "satisfice") is when a participant gives the minimum acceptable answer instead of doing the cognitive work of retrieving, integrating, and reporting accurately. Short, low-effort, non-committal answers may signal fatigue, disengagement, discomfort with the topic, or an interview that has run too long — and data collected while a participant is satisficing is low-validity data.

## Signals

- Answers become markedly shorter over the session ("yeah", "it's fine", "not really").
- Verbatim repetition of the researcher's own words back at them.
- Acquiescence streaks — several consecutive agreements without qualification.
- "Don't know" / "can't remember" used for questions the participant answered richly earlier.
- Flat prosody, longer response latency, checking the time (observable live, not in transcript).

## Causes and responses

| Likely cause | Response |
|---|---|
| Fatigue (long session) | Offer a break, or move the most important remaining questions forward |
| Topic discomfort | Acknowledge and de-escalate; return later or drop |
| Question is too abstract | Switch to a concrete incident (see Critical Incident Technique) |
| Rapport not established | Step back to easier, experience-near questions |
| Participant sees no stake | Restate why their specific experience matters |

## Detection patterns (when a coach should flag)

- Rolling average answer length drops sharply relative to the participant's own earlier baseline (within-participant comparison, not absolute length — some people are naturally terse).
- Three or more consecutive minimal answers ("yes", "no", "fine", "I guess").
- Researcher receives a minimal answer to an open question and continues down the discussion guide without adjusting.

## Coaching note

Flag with a low-key suggestion ("Answers are getting shorter — consider a break or a concrete-incident question") and the rationale: continuing to collect answers from a satisficing participant produces data that looks complete but isn't trustworthy. Compare against the participant's own baseline to avoid false positives on terse speakers.
