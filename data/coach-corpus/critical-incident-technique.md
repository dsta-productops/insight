# Critical Incident Technique (CIT)

## Principle

Anchor questions to a specific, real, recent event rather than to generalizations or hypotheticals. People are unreliable at reporting their average behaviour and worse at predicting future behaviour, but they are good at recounting a particular episode. One concrete incident, walked through in detail, yields more valid data than ten opinions. The technique dates to Flanagan (1954), developed for analysing aviation performance.

## Method

1. Elicit the incident: "Tell me about the last time you [did the task]." or "Think of a time this went badly wrong — what happened?"
2. Establish the scene: when, where, who else was involved, what was at stake.
3. Walk the timeline step by step: "What did you do first?" "Then what?"
4. Probe decisions inside the incident: "At that point, what were your options?" "What made you choose that?"
5. Capture outcome and aftermath: what happened, what they'd do differently.

## Good practice

- Prefer *recent* incidents — memory decays and reconstructs quickly.
- Prefer *specific over representative*: resist the participant's drift back into "usually I…" — gently return to "and that day, what did you do?"
- Collect both critical successes and critical failures; the technique's power is in the extremes.

## Detection patterns (when a coach should suggest this)

- Researcher asks a hypothetical: "Would you use…", "What would you do if…", "How likely would you be to…".
- Participant answers in generalities: "usually", "typically", "most of the time", "I guess I would".
- Researcher asks about frequency or habit ("how often do you…") where an incident walk-through would give richer data.
- An emotionally loaded event is mentioned in passing ("there was one time it completely failed") and not picked up.

## Example rewrite

- Hypothetical: "Would you trust the system's recommendation in a time-critical situation?"
- CIT form: "Tell me about the most recent time you had to act on a recommendation under time pressure. What did you do?"

## Coaching note

When a hypothetical is detected, suggest the concrete-incident rewrite and include the rationale: stated intentions correlate weakly with actual behaviour; a recounted incident is observable evidence.
