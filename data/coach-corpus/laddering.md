# Laddering

## Principle

Laddering probes upward from concrete attributes and actions to the consequences they produce and, ultimately, the personal goals and values they serve. It comes from means-end theory in consumer psychology (Reynolds & Gutman): attributes → consequences → values. In UX research, laddering explains *why a feature matters*, which transfers across products and contexts far better than feature-level preferences.

## Method

1. Anchor on something concrete the participant said they do, use, or prefer ("I always export to PDF").
2. Ask what that does for them ("What does exporting to PDF let you do?") — this surfaces a consequence ("I can send it to my boss without formatting breaking").
3. Ask why that consequence matters ("Why is it important that it arrives intact?") — this surfaces a higher goal ("I look careless if it's mangled") and eventually a value (professional credibility).
4. Stop when the participant reaches identity- or value-level statements, or begins repeating themselves.

## Good practice

- Ladder from *stated behaviour*, not from hypotheticals.
- One rung at a time; skipping rungs ("So this is about your reputation?") is leading and often wrong.
- Downward laddering also exists: from an abstract statement ("I need to feel in control") probe down for the concrete situations that produce it.

## Detection patterns (when a coach should suggest this)

- Participant expresses a strong preference or habit and the researcher records it without asking what it achieves for them.
- Conversation is stuck at feature level ("I like the filters", "the colours are nice") for several exchanges.
- Participant uses value-charged words ("trust", "safe", "professional", "in control") that have not been connected to concrete behaviour.

## Example ladder

- Attribute: "I use the offline mode every day."
- Consequence: "I can keep working on the train."
- Consequence: "I arrive with the report already drafted."
- Value: "I'm seen as someone who's always prepared."

## Coaching note

Suggest the next rung explicitly ("Ask what having X lets them do") and include the rationale: connecting features to goals reveals which requirements are load-bearing and which are incidental.
