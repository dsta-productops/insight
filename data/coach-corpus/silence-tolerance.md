# Silence Tolerance

## Principle

Pauses do the interviewer's work. After a question — and especially after a participant's first answer — silence creates gentle pressure that the participant usually fills with elaboration, qualification, or the thing they were deciding whether to say. Researchers who fill pauses (rephrasing the question, suggesting answers, moving on) systematically truncate their data. Conversation analysis shows typical turn-taking gaps are under one second; deliberately holding three to five seconds feels long to the researcher and generative to the participant.

## Method

1. Ask the question once, cleanly, then stop talking.
2. After the participant's answer, hold the pause a beat longer than feels natural — nodding or a soft "mm" keeps the channel open without adding content.
3. If the silence must be broken, use minimal continuers ("Say more about that?") rather than a new question.

## Why researchers fill silence

- Discomfort — silence feels like a failed question.
- Fear the participant didn't understand — leading to rephrasing that turns one question into three (question stacking).
- Time pressure — a packed discussion guide incentivizes moving on.

## Detection patterns (when a coach should flag)

- Researcher speaks again within ~2 seconds of the participant finishing.
- Researcher rephrases their own question before the participant has answered.
- Researcher supplies candidate answers ("Was it the layout, or the speed, or…?") after a short pause.
- Participant's answer ends with a hedge or trailing tone ("…so, yeah") — a strong signal that more is available if the researcher waits.

## Coaching note

The in-session suggestion should be brief ("Let this pause sit") with the rationale: participants often deliver the most candid material after the socially expected answer, and only if given room. Note for INSIGHT: this pattern is detected from timing and turn structure, so suggestions should be suppressed unless pause-filling is recurring — flagging a single short pause is noise.
